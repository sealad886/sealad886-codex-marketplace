# Research method

## Choose authority for the claim

Authority is contextual, not a universal ranking of websites.

| Claim | Useful primary evidence | Applicability to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protocol requirement | Normative standard/specification and errata | Edition, optional features, implementation conformance |
| Software behavior | Versioned official docs, release notes, upstream source | Installed version, platform, configuration, release status |
| Rule or official process | Competent regulator/public body and controlling text | Jurisdiction, effective date, exceptions, superseding changes |
| Event or historical fact | Contemporaneous primary record and independent reporting | Event date versus publication date, corrected accounts |
| Scientific result | Original study, methods/data, relevant synthesis | Population, design, effect size, uncertainty, replication |
| Product price/availability | Current official listing or provider response | Region, taxes/fees, conditions, retrieval date |

An official statement can establish what an organization claims without
independently proving the claim. Expert interpretation can explain a source but
should not silently replace the controlling record. Follow current host rules
for high-stakes topics and primary-source requirements.

## Build a small claim-to-source map

For consequential or disputed claims, retain:

- Claim and whether it is fact, assertion, inference, forecast, or recommendation.
- Source title/organization, direct URL, and consulted passage or section.
- Publication/update/event/effective dates when available; retrieval date when useful.
- Applicable version, geography, population, units, and important exclusions.
- Independent corroboration or the common original behind repeated reporting.
- Confidence, conflicting evidence, access limits, and unresolved questions.

A brief may express this directly in prose; do not impose a database or separate
artifact for a simple lookup. Do not store sensitive source contents unnecessarily.

## Verify dates and versions

"Latest" needs a relevant time and source. A recently updated web page can still
describe an old event or an unreleased feature. A cached or archived page may be
valuable historical evidence without proving current availability.

For software, distinguish current documentation from the project's actual pinned
version. Verify version-dependent APIs against applicable documentation/source.
If mismatch remains, explain it and avoid presenting untested instructions as
working commands. A cited code example is not proof it ran in the user's project.

For rules, distinguish announcement, publication, commencement/effective date,
and local applicability. Do not replace one jurisdiction's primary source with
another's general explanation.

## Handle disagreement and access failures

Check whether disagreement is real or due to different definitions, periods,
versions, populations, measurements, or incentives. Read the original evidence
where available. A dozen copied articles do not outvote one controlling record.

If disagreement remains material, present the competing supported claims,
explain the uncertainty, and identify the evidence needed to decide. Do not hide
minority credible evidence solely because it complicates the answer.

When a requested source is unavailable, record the access limit and use an
appropriate alternate if available. Cite the alternate actually read. Do not
attribute the alternate's contents to the unavailable source or bypass access
controls. Search snippets can identify a promising source but do not replace it.

## Citations and synthesis

Place citations after the supported claim or tightly scoped paragraph. Use
descriptive direct links or the host's native source citations. Each citation
must support the attached claim, including qualifications. A citation to a long
document benefits from a section/page pointer when available.

Attribute quotations accurately and follow the applicable quotation/copyright
limits. Paraphrase reasoning in original language. Do not inflate confidence by
repeating a source's language or listing many links.

An answer can conclude "evidence does not resolve this" and still satisfy a
bounded research task. State what is known, why uncertainty remains, and what
decision can responsibly follow. Avoid canned claims that all relevant evidence
has been found or that sources guarantee correctness.
